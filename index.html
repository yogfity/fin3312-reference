Module 1–2 · 4 exam questions

Foundations

The goal of financial management, business organization forms, agency conflicts, and the markets where securities trade. Mostly conceptual — fast points if you know the vocabulary.

Goal of Financial Management

The objective of the firm — and the lens through which every decision is evaluated — is to maximize the current market value of the existing equity. Equivalent phrasings: maximize shareholder wealth, maximize the stock price.

Why not these other goals?

Maximize profit: ignores timing of cash flows and risk; easy to game with accounting choices.
Maximize sales / market share: can lose money in pursuit of growth.
Maximize EPS: manipulable through buybacks and accounting.
Avoid bankruptcy: too conservative — leaves value on the table.

Forms of Business Organization

FormLiabilityTaxationLife
Sole ProprietorshipUnlimited personalPersonal income tax (once)Limited (dies with owner)
PartnershipUnlimited jointPersonal income tax (once)Limited
CorporationLimited (to investment)Double-taxed (corp + dividends)Unlimited
LLCLimitedPass-throughVariable

Trade-off in a nutshell: Corporations get limited liability, transferable ownership, and unlimited life — at the cost of double taxation. Sole props and partnerships are simple and taxed once — at the cost of unlimited personal liability and a finite life.

The Agency Problem

A conflict of interest between principals (shareholders, who own the firm) and agents (managers, who run it). Managers may pursue perks, empire-building, job security, or short-term results at the expense of long-term shareholder value.

Mechanisms to align interests

  • Stock-based compensation — managers get paid in equity, so their wealth tracks the share price
  • Threat of takeover — poorly-run firms become acquisition targets, and bad managers get replaced
  • Threat of firing by the board of directors
  • Monitoring by large shareholders and institutional investors

Agency costs are the costs that arise from this conflict — direct (monitoring expenses) and indirect (lost value from poor decisions).

Financial Markets

DistinctionDefinition
Primary marketWhere new securities are issued (IPOs, new bond offerings). Money flows to the company.
Secondary marketWhere existing securities trade between investors. The company gets nothing — it's investor-to-investor.
Money marketShort-term debt (under 1 year): T-bills, commercial paper
Capital marketLong-term debt and equity: bonds, stocks
Auction marketBrokers match buyers and sellers. NYSE.
Dealer market (OTC)Dealers buy/sell from inventory. NASDAQ.

Bankruptcy Priority

When a company is liquidated, claimants are paid in this strict order:

  1. Senior debt (secured creditors first)
  2. Bonds / unsecured creditors
  3. Preferred stockholders
  4. Common stockholders — residual claimants, get whatever's left (usually nothing)
Common stock = lowest priority. This is why equity is riskier than debt: in bankruptcy, equity holders are last in line, and the line usually doesn't reach them.
Module 3 · 3 exam questions

Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. All of finance is just translating cash flows between points in time using a discount rate.

The Five TVM Variables

Every TVM problem revolves around five variables. You're always given four and solving for the fifth.

VariableMeaningTI-84 Key
NNumber of periodsN
I/YInterest rate per periodI%
PVPresent value (today)PV
PMTRecurring paymentPMT
FVFuture valueFV
Sign convention
Money out of your pocket is negative. Money in is positive. The calculator enforces this — if you invest $1,000 today (money out), enter PV as −1000. The signs are bookkeeping; report final answers as positive.

The Four Problem Types

1. Single lump sum (no PMT)

Invest $X today, what's it worth in N years? Or: I need $X in N years, how much today?

TI-84: PMT = 0, solve for PV or FV.

2. Annuity (level PMT, no lump sum)

Save $X per year for N years. Or: receive $X per year for N years.

TI-84: Either PV or FV = 0, solve for the other.

3. Mixed (PMT + lump sum)

Both a stream and a final/initial lump sum. This is what bond pricing is. PMT = coupons, FV = face value. Same machinery, different domain.

4. Uneven cash flows

Different cash flows each period. TVM Solver doesn't work — use the npv( function.

Perpetuities

An annuity that lasts forever.

Zero-growth perpetuity PV = C / r Growing perpetuity (Gordon model) PV = C₁ / (r − g)

The growing perpetuity formula is the bones of the constant-growth stock model — same equation, different name.

EAR vs APR

APR (stated rate) is the rate as advertised. It doesn't account for compounding frequency.

EAR (effective annual rate) is the true rate after compounding within the year. EAR ≥ APR always.

Effective annual rate EAR = (1 + APR/m)^m − 1
Compoundingm
Annual1
Semiannual2
Quarterly4
Monthly12
Daily365
Continuous compounding EAR = e^APR − 1
The compounding trick
"12% compounded monthly" doesn't mean you earn 12% a year. You earn 1% a month, which compounds to 12.68% effective. Two loans both quoted at "12% APR" cost different amounts depending on compounding frequency.

Rule of 72

Mental-math shortcut for doubling time:

Years to double ≈ 72 / r

At 8%, money doubles in ~9 years. Useful for sanity checks.

Annuity Due vs Ordinary Annuity

Ordinary annuity (END): payment at the end of each period. This is the default — most loans, bonds, retirement contributions.

Annuity due (BGN): payment at the beginning of each period. Used for leases, rent, some insurance.

TI-84: Switch to BGN mode
  1. 2ND then PMT (opens BEGIN/END menu)
  2. Highlight BGN
  3. ENTER
  4. 2ND then MODE to exit
Always reset to END after a BGN problem. Otherwise you'll silently get wrong answers on every subsequent ordinary annuity problem.
Module 4 · 7 exam questions

Bonds

A bond is a loan you make to a company or government. They pay you periodic coupon interest and return the face value at maturity. Every bond problem reduces to the same TVM machinery: annuity of coupons plus lump sum at maturity.

Anatomy of a Bond

Five variables, five questions. A bond problem gives you four — find the fifth.

VariableWhat it represents
NPeriods to maturity
I/YYield to maturity per period
PMTCoupon payment per period (a dollar amount)
FVFace value (almost always $1,000)
PVBond price today
Coupon payment PMT = Face Value × Coupon Rate
Coupon rate ≠ YTM
The coupon rate is fixed in the bond contract forever. The YTM is the market rate, which changes daily. They're equal only at issuance (and rarely thereafter).

Price vs Yield Relationship

RelationshipBond is calledWhy
Coupon = YTMPar bond (price = $1,000)Coupon exactly compensates for the market rate
Coupon > YTMPremium bond (price > $1,000)Bond pays more than market demands — worth extra
Coupon < YTMDiscount bond (price < $1,000)Bond pays less than market demands — sells cheap

The fundamental inverse: Interest rates and bond prices move in opposite directions. Rates up → prices down.

Semiannual Setup (The Most Common Variant)

Most U.S. corporate bonds pay coupons twice a year. When a problem says "semiannual" or "pays interest twice a year":

  • N = years × 2 (number of 6-month periods)
  • I/Y = annual YTM ÷ 2 (per-period rate)
  • PMT = annual coupon ÷ 2
  • FV = $1,000 (unchanged)
  • If solving for I/Y, the answer is semiannual — multiply by 2 to get the annual YTM

The Four Bond Question Types

Find bond price (given everything else)

TI-84 workflow
  1. Enter N, I/Y, PMT, FV
  2. CPT PV
  3. Drop the negative sign — that's calculator convention for outflow

Find YTM (given price)

TI-84 workflow
  1. Enter N, PV (negative, since you pay it), PMT, FV
  2. CPT I/Y
  3. If semiannual: multiply by 2 for annual YTM

Find coupon rate (given price and YTM)

TI-84 workflow
  1. Enter N, I/Y, PV (negative), FV
  2. CPT PMT → gives per-period coupon in dollars
  3. Annual bond: rate = PMT / Face
  4. Semiannual bond: rate = (PMT × 2) / Face

Find time to maturity

TI-84 workflow
  1. Enter I/Y, PV (negative), PMT, FV
  2. CPT N
  3. If semiannual: divide N by 2 for years

Current Yield

Current Yield = Annual Coupon / Bond Price

This measures only the income return — it ignores the capital gain or loss from the bond price drifting toward par at maturity. YTM captures the total return.

  • Discount bond: Current Yield < YTM (you also gain as price rises to $1,000)
  • Premium bond: Current Yield > YTM (you lose as price falls to $1,000)
  • Par bond: Current Yield = YTM = coupon rate

Types of Bond Risk

RiskDescription
Interest rate riskBond prices fall when rates rise. Bigger impact on long-maturity, low-coupon bonds.
Default (credit) riskIssuer fails to make coupon or principal payments. Higher for low-rated bonds; near-zero for U.S. Treasuries.
Inflation riskReal return is eroded if inflation exceeds expectations.
Reinvestment riskEarly coupons may have to be reinvested at lower rates if market rates fall.
Liquidity riskBond can't be sold quickly without a price discount. Worst for thinly-traded corporates.

Interest rate risk drivers

  • Longer maturity → more interest rate risk. A 30-year bond moves much more than a 2-year bond when rates change.
  • Lower coupon → more interest rate risk. Zero-coupon bonds are the most rate-sensitive of all.

Credit Ratings

TierS&PMoody's
Investment gradeAAA → AA → A → BBB-Aaa → Aa → A → Baa3
Speculative ("junk" / high-yield)BB+ → BB → B → CCC → ... → DBa1 → Ba → B → Caa → Ca → C

The cutoff line: BBB- (Baa3) is the lowest investment grade. BB+ (Ba1) is the highest junk grade. Many institutional investors (pensions, insurance) are prohibited from holding speculative-grade debt — so falling below BBB- is a big deal for an issuer.

Risk and Return

How we measure risk, how we compensate investors for bearing it, and which risks investors actually get paid to take.

Standard Deviation = Total Risk

Standard deviation measures the average size of a stock's return swings from its mean. It captures every source of return variation — both market-wide and firm-specific. That's why it's called "total" risk.

Note Variance (σ²) = Standard Deviation squared

Same idea as SD, just harder to interpret because the units are squared.

Systematic vs Unsystematic Risk

Systematic RiskUnsystematic Risk
Also calledMarket risk / non-diversifiableFirm-specific / idiosyncratic / diversifiable
AffectsThe entire marketOne company or industry
ExamplesRecession, rates, inflation, warLawsuit, scandal, earnings miss, factory fire
Diversifiable?NoYes
Measured byBeta (β)—
Compensated?Yes — risk premiumNo — diversifiable for free

Why Diversification Works

Holding one stock means bearing both systematic and unsystematic risk. As you add more stocks:

  • Unsystematic risk averages out — different firms experience different events that cancel each other
  • Systematic risk stays — every stock responds to the same macro events

At roughly 30–40 stocks, unsystematic risk approaches zero. What's left is systematic risk — the floor you can't get below.

Why CAPM uses β and not SD
The market only pays you for risk you can't avoid. Since unsystematic risk is diversifiable for free, you don't get a premium for bearing it. Beta isolates the systematic portion — which is what determines required return.

Beta

A measure of how much a stock moves with the market. The market itself has β = 1 by definition.

  • β = 1: moves with the market
  • β > 1: more volatile than the market (amplifies moves)
  • β < 1: less volatile than the market
  • β < 0: moves opposite to the market (rare — gold mining stocks sometimes)

CAPM

The Capital Asset Pricing Model. Tells you what return investors require for a given stock, based on its systematic risk.

Required return (CAPM) r = r_f + β × (r_m − r_f)
  • r_f = risk-free rate (typically Treasury yield)
  • r_m = expected market return
  • (r_m − r_f) = market risk premium — the extra return for bearing market risk
  • β × (r_m − r_f) = stock risk premium — your stock's individual share of that
Combined CAPM + Gordon
Moe's Tavern
P₀ = $175, β = 1.5, D₀ = $2.50, r_f = 3%, market premium = 6%. Find expected g.

Step 1 — CAPM: r = 0.03 + 1.5(0.06) = 12%

Step 2 — solve for g (D₀ given): g = (175 × 0.12 − 2.50) / (175 + 2.50) = 18.50 / 177.50 = 10.42%

Portfolio Math

For a portfolio of multiple stocks, expected return and beta are both weighted averages.

Expected portfolio return E(R_p) = Σ w_i × E(R_i) Portfolio beta β_p = Σ w_i × β_i

The weights are dollar weights — fraction of total portfolio value invested in each asset.

Stock Valuation

The heart of the course. Every stock model is a special case of "discount the future cash flows back to today." The art is identifying which model fits the wording of the problem.

Constant Growth (Gordon Model)

The workhorse formula. Assumes dividends grow at a constant rate forever.

Current stock price P₀ = D₁ / (r − g)

Critical: the numerator is D₁ (next year's dividend), not D₀. If the problem gives you D₀ ("just paid"), grow it first: D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g).

When the formula breaks
The Gordon model requires r > g. If g ≥ r, the math says price = ∞, which means the formula doesn't apply — you can't actually grow faster than your discount rate forever.

Future Stock Price

In constant growth, the stock price grows at the same rate g as the dividends. So two equivalent ways to find the price t years out:

Shortcut P_t = P₀ × (1 + g)^t Long way (same answer) P_t = D_(t+1) / (r − g)

Notice in the long way, the dividend is from year t+1 — Gordon plants the price one year before the dividend in the numerator. This becomes critical in deferred-dividend problems.

Zero Growth / Preferred Stock

Constant dividend forever, no growth. Same as a perpetuity.

Price of zero-growth stock P₀ = D / r

Preferred stock uses this formula too — preferred dividends are typically fixed.

Solving for r

Algebraic rearrangement of Gordon:

Required return r = D₁/P₀ + g

Two pieces with real meaning:

  • D₁/P₀ = dividend yield (the income return)
  • g = capital gains yield (price growth, equal to dividend growth)
  • Total required return = dividend yield + capital gains yield

Solving for g

Two formulas, depending on what the problem gives you.

Case 1: D₁ given

Simple rearrangement.

If D₁ is provided g = r − D₁/P₀

Case 2: D₀ given

Harder because g appears in both numerator and denominator. Cross-multiply, distribute, collect g terms.

If D₀ is provided (just-paid dividend) g = (P₀ × r − D₀) / (D₀ + P₀)
Derivation

Starting from P₀ = D₀(1+g)/(r−g):

  1. Cross-multiply: P₀(r−g) = D₀(1+g)
  2. Distribute: P₀r − P₀g = D₀ + D₀g
  3. Collect g terms: P₀r − D₀ = D₀g + P₀g
  4. Factor: P₀r − D₀ = g(D₀ + P₀)
  5. Solve: g = (P₀r − D₀) / (D₀ + P₀)

Non-Constant Growth (Multi-Stage)

Dividends grow at one rate for a while, then settle into constant growth. The classic exam-trap problem type.

Universal template

  1. Build each dividend year by year through the irregular phase: D₁, D₂, … D_n
  2. Compute the first dividend in the constant-growth phase: D_(n+1) = D_n × (1 + g)
  3. Compute the terminal price at the end of the irregular phase: P_n = D_(n+1) / (r − g)
  4. The year-n cash flow becomes D_n + P_n (dividend plus terminal value)
  5. NPV all cash flows back to today
The #1 trap on non-constant growth
The terminal price P_n sits at the end of the irregular dividend period — the same year as the last irregular dividend. Then the first growing dividend is the next year. Putting P_n in the wrong year is the most common error students make on this entire chapter.
Two-stage growth
Rahm Corporation
D₀ = $3.70, g = 28% for 3 years then 7.9% forever, r = 16%.

D₁ = 4.74, D₂ = 6.06, D₃ = 7.76, D₄ = 8.37

P₃ = 8.37 / (0.16 − 0.079) = $103.36

Year 3 cash flow = 7.76 + 103.36 = $111.12

NPV(16, 0, {4.74, 6.06, 111.12}) → $78.65

"Double each year" growth
Dividend doubles for 4 years
D₀ = $0.50, doubles for 4 years, then 5% constant, r = 9.5%.

D₁ = 1.00, D₂ = 2.00, D₃ = 4.00, D₄ = 8.00, D₅ = 8.40

P₄ = 8.40 / 0.045 = $186.67. Year 4 CF = 8.00 + 186.67 = 194.67

NPV(9.5, 0, {1, 2, 4, 194.67}) → $141.03

Deferred Dividends

Company doesn't pay anything for several years, then starts paying. Sometimes the first dividend kicks off constant growth.

The recipe

  1. Find the dividend that starts the constant growth phase, D_t
  2. Apply Gordon: P_(t−1) = D_t / (r − g) — this gives the price one year before the first dividend
  3. Discount that price back to today using N = t − 1
Deferred-then-growing
Metallica Bearings
No dividends for 11 years. First dividend at year 12 = $12.50. Then 5.5% growth forever. r = 13.5%.

P₁₁ = 12.50 / (0.135 − 0.055) = $156.25

Discount back 11 years: 156.25 / (1.135)^11 = $38.07

Finite Dividend (Stops Forever)

Stock pays a fixed dividend for X years, then stops. This is not a perpetuity — it's a finite annuity.

TI-84 workflow
  1. N = years of dividend payments
  2. I/Y = required return
  3. PMT = dividend amount
  4. FV = 0 (stock is worthless after dividends stop)
  5. CPT PV → price today
Finite annuity
Hailey Corporation
$14.50 constant dividend for 8 years, then ceases forever. r = 10%.

N=8, I=10, PMT=14.50, FV=0 → PV = $77.36. Not $145 (which would be a perpetuity).

Valuation by Multiples

P/E (Price/Earnings) valuation

Stock Price = EPS × P/E ratio

Used when comparing to peer multiples. Find a "benchmark" P/E from comparable firms, apply to your firm's EPS.

P/S (Price/Sales) valuation

Stock Price = (Sales / Shares outstanding) × P/S ratio

Used when earnings are negative (a young or distressed company) and P/E can't be calculated. Same idea, different denominator.

D₀ vs D₁ — Reading the Wording

Half the battle on stock problems is figuring out whether the dividend you're given is D₀ or D₁.

"just paid" / "recently paid"
D₀ — grow forward: D₁ = D₀(1+g)
"annual dividend of $X"
D₀ (no time clue) — grow forward
"next dividend"
D₁ — use directly
"will pay next year"
D₁ — use directly
"expected dividend"
D₁ — use directly
"current dividend" (in answer)
D₀ — if you computed D₁, back it out: D₀ = D₁ / (1+g)
The most common stock-problem error
Mixing up D₀ and D₁. If you blank, ask: "Has this money already been paid?" If yes, D₀ — grow it. If no, D₁ — use it.

Capital Budgeting

Should the firm take this project? Five tools to answer that question — and a clear hierarchy of which one to trust when they disagree.

NPV — The Gold Standard

Net Present Value: the dollar value the project adds to the firm today, after discounting all future cash flows at the required return.

Decision rule Accept if NPV > 0; reject if NPV < 0
TI-84 — NPV function
  1. APPS → Finance → npv(
  2. Syntax: npv(rate%, −CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, ...})
  3. Rate as whole number (10 not 0.10)
  4. CF₀ entered as negative (initial outflow)
  5. Subsequent cash flows in curly braces

IRR — The Project's Built-In Return

Internal Rate of Return: the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. Compare it to the required return to decide.

Decision rule Accept if IRR > required return; reject if IRR < required return
TI-84 — IRR function
  1. APPS → Finance → irr(
  2. Syntax: irr(−CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, ...})
  3. No rate argument — you're solving for it
  4. Output is already a percentage
"Indifferent" = "NPV = 0" = IRR
When a problem asks "at what discount rate would you be indifferent between accepting and rejecting?" — they're asking for the IRR. Three phrasings, one calculation.

NPV vs IRR — When They Disagree

For independent projects, NPV and IRR always give the same accept/reject decision. For mutually exclusive projects (where you can only pick one), they can rank projects differently.

When they conflict, always trust NPV. IRR has two known weaknesses: it assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself (unrealistic for high-IRR projects) and it can produce multiple values for projects with sign changes.

Other Capital Budgeting Methods

Payback Period

How many years until cumulative cash flows recover the initial investment. Simple and intuitive but ignores time value and any cash flows after the payback point. Inferior to NPV.

Discounted Payback

Same as payback but discount each cash flow first. Better than plain payback but still ignores cash flows after the payback point.

Profitability Index (PI)

Formula PI = PV of future cash flows / |CF₀|

Decision rule: accept if PI > 1. Useful when capital is constrained and you need to rank projects by "bang for buck."

MIRR (Modified IRR)

Addresses IRR's reinvestment-rate problem by explicitly assuming intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the required return (not at IRR). More realistic than plain IRR.

Worked examples

Pure NPV
Project with three years of cash flows
CF₀ = −$151,000; Year 1 = $65,000; Year 2 = $74,000; Year 3 = $58,000. Required return: 11% and 22%.

At 11%: npv(11, -151000, {65000, 74000, 58000}) → $11,758.27 ✓ accept

At 22%: npv(22, -151000, {65000, 74000, 58000}) → −$15,512.97 ✗ reject

IRR + decision
CF₀ = −$146,000; Years 1–3 = $70K, $69K, $53K

irr(-146000, {70000, 69000, 53000}) → 15.04%

Appendix A1

TI-84 Quick Reference

Every keystroke from the course in one place.

TVM Solver

The workhorse for any problem with level cash flows. APPS → Finance → TVM Solver.

  • N — number of periods
  • I% — interest rate per period (as a percent, not decimal)
  • PV — present value (negative for money out)
  • PMT — recurring payment
  • FV — future value
  • P/Y, C/Y — keep both at 1 unless you're doing the manual semiannual conversion shortcut
  • PMT: END — ordinary annuity (default). Switch to BGN for annuity due.

To solve, scroll to the unknown variable and press ALPHA then ENTER (the SOLVE shortcut).

NPV function

Syntax npv(rate%, −CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, …})
  • Rate as whole number (10 not 0.10)
  • CF₀ negative for initial outflow
  • Other cash flows in curly braces, comma-separated

IRR function

Syntax irr(−CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, …})
  • No rate argument — you're solving for it
  • Output already in percent

Common calculator traps

  1. Sign mismatches — if money goes out today (investment), PV must be negative. Sign mismatches give nonsense answers or error messages.
  2. BGN mode stuck on — always reset to END after annuity-due problems.
  3. P/Y leftover from previous problem — default to 1 unless you have a reason.
  4. Decimal vs percent — I% takes the number as a percent (10 = 10%), not a decimal. Don't enter 0.10.
  5. Rounding intermediate values — McGraw-Hill grades to the penny. Store unrounded values in memory or carry 4+ decimals between steps.

Appendix A4

Two-Step Patterns

The single most common exam structure: compute an intermediate value, then feed that into a second formula. Recognize the pattern and the problem solves itself.

Retirement (PV becomes the FV)

Two distinct phases: accumulation (savings) and withdrawal (retirement). The lump sum you need at the start of retirement is the PV of withdrawals and the FV target of savings — same number, two perspectives.

  1. Find PV of withdrawals as of retirement date
  2. That PV becomes the FV target for the savings phase
  3. Solve PMT to reach that FV

Mortgage interest in a window

  1. Find the monthly payment using the full loan: N, I/Y, PV, FV=0 → CPT PMT
  2. Find the loan balance at the start of the window: N = months remaining, PMT, FV=0 → CPT PV
  3. Total paid in window = PMT × months. Principal paid = starting balance − ending balance. Interest = total − principal.

Deferred annuity

  1. Compute PV of the annuity stream as of one period before the first payment
  2. Discount that PV back to today using N = years until that point

Non-constant growth stock

  1. Build all irregular dividends year by year
  2. Compute the first constant-growth dividend
  3. P_n = D_(n+1) / (r − g) at the LAST irregular year
  4. Add P_n to D_n to form the year-n cash flow
  5. NPV everything back to today

CAPM-then-stock

  1. Use CAPM to find r: r = r_f + β(r_m − r_f)
  2. Use r in whatever stock formula the problem requires (Gordon, solve-for-g, etc.)

Bond future price

  1. If YTM isn't given: find it from current price, coupon, and maturity
  2. Re-price the bond with the remaining years
Stock Problem

Constant Growth (Gordon)

Use when the problem says dividends grow at a constant rate forever.

P₀ = D₁ / (r − g)

Watch the D₀ vs D₁ wording. "Just paid" = D₀, grow it. "Next year" = D₁, use directly.

Stock Problem

Zero Growth / Preferred Stock

Use when "constant dividend forever" with no growth. Same formula as a perpetuity.

P₀ = D / r
Stock Problem

Non-Constant Growth

See full template in Module 6.

  1. Build irregular dividends
  2. D_(n+1) = first constant-growth dividend
  3. P_n = D_(n+1)/(r−g) at last irregular year
  4. Year n CF = D_n + P_n
  5. NPV back to today
Stock Problem

Deferred Dividends

  1. Find the first dividend (year t)
  2. P_(t−1) = D_t / (r − g)
  3. Discount back t−1 years
Stock Problem

Finite Dividend (Stops Forever)

"Constant dividend for X years, then ceases" → TVM annuity, not a perpetuity.

TVM Solver: N=years, PMT=dividend, FV=0, solve PV.

Stock Problem

Solving for g

Case 1 (D₁ given): g = r − D₁/P₀

Case 2 (D₀ given): g = (P₀r − D₀) / (D₀ + P₀)

Stock Problem

Solving for r

r = D₁/P₀ + g

Or: dividend yield + capital gains yield. If only D₀ is given, grow it first.

Stock Problem

PE / P-S Valuation

PE: Price = EPS × P/E

P/S: Price = (Sales / Shares) × P/S — use when earnings are negative.

Bond Problem

Bond Pricing

TVM Solver: enter N, I/Y, PMT, FV=1000, solve PV. Semiannual: N×2, I/Y÷2, PMT÷2.

Bond Problem

Solving for YTM

TVM Solver: N, PV (negative), PMT, FV=1000, CPT I/Y. Semiannual: multiply answer by 2.

Bond Problem

Solving for Coupon Rate

TVM Solver: N, I/Y, PV (negative), FV=1000, CPT PMT. Then:

Annual: rate = PMT / Face

Semiannual: rate = (PMT × 2) / Face

Bond Problem

Future Bond Price

  1. Find YTM from current price (if not given)
  2. Re-price with remaining years to maturity
Capital Budgeting

NPV

npv(rate%, −CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, …})

Accept if NPV > 0.

Capital Budgeting

IRR

irr(−CF₀, {CF₁, CF₂, …})

Accept if IRR > required return.

Capital Budgeting

Payback / PI / MIRR

Payback: years to recover CF₀ (no time value).

Profitability Index: PV(future CFs) / |CF₀|; accept if > 1.

MIRR: reinvests at required return, not IRR; more realistic.

TVM Pattern

Two-Step Retirement

  1. PV of withdrawals = amount needed at retirement
  2. That PV becomes the FV target for savings phase
  3. Solve PMT to reach FV from today

The key insight: PV becomes the FV.

TVM Pattern

Mortgage Amortization

  1. Find monthly payment: full loan TVM, CPT PMT
  2. Find balance at the desired window: N = remaining months, PMT, FV=0, CPT PV
  3. Interest in window = (PMT × months) − (balance change)
TVM Pattern

Deferred Annuity

  1. PV of the annuity = value one period before first payment
  2. Discount that PV back to today
TVM Pattern

EAR vs APR

EAR = (1 + APR/m)^m − 1

m: annual=1, semi=2, quarterly=4, monthly=12, daily=365.

Continuous: EAR = e^APR − 1
